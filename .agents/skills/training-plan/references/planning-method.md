# Planning method and evidence

Use for creating or substantively reviewing cycling plans. Evidence checked
2026-11-17. This is a bounded decision reference, not an exhaustive literature
review. Study findings below support options; they do not establish an optimal
prescription for an individual. The workflow and decision criteria are practical
coaching rules unless explicitly attributed to a study.

## Establish the training problem

Record the performance objective and date, expected event duration, terrain,
decisive effort lengths, repeated-effort demands and the importance of performing
after prolonged riding. For general fitness, define a measurable objective and
review horizon without inventing a competition.

Summarize training age, recent consistent training, interruptions, tolerated
volume and quality frequency, available days/hours, other sport/strength work
and practical recovery constraints. Choose a representative history window and
state coverage; an unusually large week is not the sustainable baseline.
Keep personal defaults in the athlete's configuration or plan, not this skill.

Map each priority as: **event demand -> observed limitation or hypothesis ->
training stimulus -> outcome measure**. For example, loss of late-ride power
requires a comparable late-ride assessment; fresh short-duration power alone
cannot establish improvement in that limitation. Check pacing, fueling, heat
and measurement differences before attributing a poor result to fitness.
Use a provisional assumption or a targeted assessment when evidence is missing;
do not invent a weakness from an Xert focus label or device estimate.

## Design the whole week and block

State planned hours, session count, quality frequency and work duration, longest
ride, recovery placement and other demanding training. Explain the change from
recent tolerated training and the expected cost for the following sessions.
Count races, tests and hard group rides as demands when they actually are hard.
A long easy ride can also materially affect the next day.

Describe the intensity distribution with its measurement method: time in power
zones, heart-rate zones and session-goal counts are different denominators.
Give zone boundaries, their source/date and uncertainty. Do not assume FTP,
Xert TP, VT2 and a fixed lactate concentration are interchangeable. Repository
session labels such as `vt1` and `vt2` are not automatically the zone definitions
used by a paper. Explain how the selected workload maps to the intended domain.

Choose polarized, pyramidal, threshold-containing or mixed organization for the
athlete's needs and response, not a compulsory 80/20 quota. Neal et al. found
larger adaptations with their polarized than threshold-heavy intervention in
12 trained male cyclists over six weeks; this comparison does not establish
that all threshold work is ineffective or that one split fits every athlete.
[Neal et al., 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23264537/).

Explain the purpose and sequence of each phase, what is developed versus
maintained, and how the emphasis becomes relevant to the goal. Block and
traditional periodization are options: an earlier small cycling trial reported
effects favoring block organization, while a later 12-week load-matched trial
found no performance advantage for either organization. Do not claim universal
superiority or equate absence of a detected difference with proven equivalence.
[Rønnestad et al., 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23134196/),
[Almquist et al., 2022](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/physiology/articles/10.3389/fphys.2022.837634/full).

## Make progression conditional and reviewable

For each priority stimulus, state the achieved anchor, proposed dose, variable
being changed (duration, repetitions, power, recovery or frequency), supporting
evidence and next review. Prefer one principal change at a time so response is
interpretable; this is a coaching heuristic, not a physiological law. If changing
several variables, justify the combined change and its effect on the week.

Duration and intensity interact: a randomized cycling study comparing interval
formats found the largest overall gains in its 4 x 8-minute group. It does not
establish that longer, harder or that specific format is always better.
[Seiler et al., 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21812820/).

Define athlete-specific evidence for these decisions in the plan:

| Decision | Evidence to assess | Required plan consequence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Increase | Intended stimulus achieved with acceptable execution, perceived effort and recovery; next sessions remain feasible | Name the increment and reassessment point; do not increase because the calendar advanced |
| Repeat | Dose remains useful; evidence for increasing is insufficient or consolidation is intended | State what the repeat will establish |
| Reduce or change | Repeated deterioration, unusual effort/recovery, illness or a documented practical constraint | Identify the cause or uncertainty, change the relevant demand and define reassessment |

Completion is evidence of tolerating a particular execution, not proof of
adaptation or automatic permission to increase. A failed session of unknown
cause does not establish a new lower capacity. Preserve achieved history and
separate temporary prescription changes from a lasting anchor change.

Plan recovery opportunities around accumulated response, event demands and
life constraints. If choosing a fixed recovery-week rhythm, label and justify
it as a planning convention. Do not treat a universal weekly percentage increase
or a fixed loading/recovery cycle as a proven safe or optimal rule. Missed work
is not a debt to cram into the next week. Use training-analysis for current
readiness and its illness-return workflow after sickness.

## Plan the event taper explicitly

For an important event, specify taper start, the reference training period,
volume change, retained intensity exposures, frequency and final demanding
session. Explain the choice using prior taper response, accumulated fatigue,
event demands and logistics. Retaining intensity does not mean retaining all
interval repetitions or the full quality-session load.

A small cycling experiment found improved 20-km performance after its seven-day
taper with a 50% volume reduction and maintained intensity. Treat this as one
tested protocol, not a mandatory duration or reduction for every rider/event.
[Neary et al., 2003](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12904635/).
For general training without a target event, explain recovery planning instead
of inventing a race taper.

## Evaluate adaptation, not just adherence

Set the review date before the block starts, matched to its purpose and duration.
Name a primary performance measure, baseline/date and repeatable protocol; add
supporting measures only if they can change the decision. Examples include a
relevant time-trial duration, repeat-effort performance or power after a defined
preload. Standardize equipment, calibration, fatigue, fueling and environment
as far as practical, and record material differences.

State the intended direction or useful magnitude of change and how measurement
noise will be handled. Use known test reliability or the athlete's repeated
results when available; otherwise report uncertainty and seek confirmation
rather than inventing a precise significance threshold. Heart rate, RPE and
device estimates can support interpretation but do not alone prove improved
race performance. Training load and completed-session counts measure exposure.

Before seeing the outcome, write the block decision rules:

- Continue or progress when relevant performance/response evidence supports
  the aim and the workload remains sustainable.
- When performance is unchanged, first assess adherence, test comparability,
  fatigue and whether the block was long enough to evaluate its intended aim.
  Decide whether to repeat, gather better evidence or change the stimulus;
  do not automatically add volume or intensity.
- When performance or recovery deteriorates, assess accumulated fatigue and
  confounders and state the recovery or plan adjustment plus reassessment date.

Report planned versus completed exposure, performance change, recovery cost,
uncertainty and the resulting decision separately. These review rules are
coaching practice; the cited group trials do not validate individual numeric
progression or stop thresholds.

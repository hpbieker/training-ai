# Cycling Endurance Physiology

Use this reference for cycling-specific concepts that generic training models
often flatten into fixed zones, a single readiness score, or total load. It
covers durability, critical power and intermittent severe-intensity work,
thermal strain, and carbohydrate availability as analysis context.

Do not combine these concepts into one fatigue score or infer a causal limiter
from an ordinary ride.

## Durability And Fatigue Resistance

Durability is the time of onset and magnitude of deterioration in a
physiological or performance characteristic during prolonged exercise. It is
not synonymous with long-ride duration, cardiac drift, a source model's
fatigue-resistance field, or merely being able to finish a ride.

- Treat fresh VT1, VT2, critical power, power-duration, and zone anchors as
  starting-state measurements. Do not assume the boundaries remain fixed after
  substantial accumulated work.
- Assess late-session cost at matched or closely comparable power. Prefer
  windowed changes in HR/W, BR/W, VE/W, RPE, SmO2 pattern and recovery, and
  thermal state over raw first-half versus second-half averages.
- Interpret HR decoupling as one marker, not durability itself. Heat,
  dehydration, carbohydrate availability, cadence, terrain, stops, posture,
  and sensor quality can produce or obscure decoupling.
- When a protocol is sufficiently standardized, report both the onset of
  meaningful deterioration and its magnitude. Use the athlete's repeated
  distribution instead of importing a universal percentage cutoff.
- Separate fresh performance from performance after prior work. A late quality
  effort or fatigued threshold assessment measures a different capability from
  the same effort performed fresh.
- Credit a long aerobic session for a durability purpose when power remains
  controlled and physiological deterioration is delayed or reduced, even when
  it does not advance a VT2 or VO2Max progression.
- Do not claim improved durability from one favorable ride. Require comparable
  prior work, power, fueling, environment, equipment and sensor placement, and
  body state, plus repeated evidence beyond expected measurement variation.
- Use durability to inform long-ride progression and late-session intensity,
  but do not turn every endurance ride into a test or compromise the next
  quality opportunity.

Cycling evidence supports durability as a distinct performance characteristic.
After 150 minutes of moderate cycling, trained cyclists showed group-average
reductions in VT1 power and five-minute performance, with the changes associated
across individuals. Later work found that HR and respiratory-frequency
decoupling at fixed work rates could help predict within-session reduction in
VT1 power. These are protocol-specific group results, not universal personal
thresholds or a validated field diagnosis.

### Late-Session Intensity Preservation

For sessions longer than 120-150 minutes, do not assume that a fixed
fresh-state power remains in the same physiological domain throughout the
ride. Give the fresh-state power as an initial range, define a reassessment
point after 120-150 minutes, and allow power to fall when needed to preserve
the intended domain. Do not instruct the athlete to recover a falling average
power or complete a source-model load target by raising late-session intensity.

At the reassessment point, compare matched steady work using HR/W, BR/W,
source-reported VE/W, SmO2 pattern and recovery, cadence, posture, temperature,
cooling, fueling, hydration, and body feel when those inputs are available and
reliable. Use repeated personal matched-session evidence rather than universal
drift cutoffs. When several signals deteriorate together, reduce power,
shorten the remaining duration, or end the session; leave omitted model dose
unscheduled rather than treating it as training debt.

## Critical Power, W Prime, And Intermittent Work

- Keep critical power (CP), functional threshold power, maximal lactate steady
  state, VT2, and Xert Threshold Power distinct. They can be related estimates
  without being interchangeable numbers.
- Treat CP as a power-duration model boundary between heavy and severe
  exercise, and W prime (`W'`) as its finite work-capacity parameter above CP.
  Do not describe W prime as directly measured anaerobic energy.
- Evaluate severe intervals from work power and duration, recovery power and
  duration, repetition count, and accumulated prior work together. Average
  power or percent of FTP alone does not specify the stimulus.
- W-prime expenditure and reconstitution models are pacing and feasibility
  evidence. Reconstitution is nonlinear, context-dependent, and individually
  variable; a generic time constant is not ground truth.
- Do not assume CP/W-prime, percent of peak power, percent of FTP, or a fixed
  delta above threshold normalizes interval tolerance between athletes.
  Calibrate against this athlete's completed structure, mechanics, respiratory
  response, SmO2 pattern, RPE, and recovery.
- Xert MPA and strain remain source-owned model outputs. They may provide a
  richer individualized strain path, but do not prove CP, W prime, oxygen
  uptake, or the physiological cause of failure.

## Cadence, Torque, And Local Mechanical Demand

Use cadence and crank torque as context when they can change the interpretation
or prescription. Do not turn ordinary activity analysis into a generic search
for an optimal cadence.

- The same external power can be produced through different combinations of
  cadence and torque. Lower cadence at the same power requires greater average
  crank torque per revolution; it is not the same local mechanical task.
- When power and cadence are time-aligned and valid, average crank torque can be
  derived as `power_w / (2 * pi * cadence_rpm / 60)`. Do not calculate it during
  zero/near-zero cadence, from mismatched averaging windows, or as pedal force
  without crank-length and force-direction information.
- Check cadence and torque context when a session has unexpected local fatigue,
  soreness, SmO2 behavior, physiological cost, power fade, or a difference from
  an otherwise comparable session.
- For matched-work comparisons, disclose a material cadence difference rather
  than attributing all HR, respiration, SmO2, or RPE change to fitness.
- A spontaneous late-session cadence decline can support a durability/fatigue
  interpretation when it aligns with controlled power and rising internal cost.
  It is not proof by itself: gearing, gradient, coasting, traffic, positioning,
  intentional cadence choice, and sensor quality must be checked.
- Do not call imposed low-cadence cycling equivalent to gym strength training,
  and do not recommend it automatically for strength, torque, threshold, or
  climbing development. Published training evidence does not establish a
  general performance advantage over freely chosen cadence.
- Prescribe a cadence target only for a concrete purpose such as reproducing a
  known event demand, testing a personal response, controlling a comparison, or
  executing a plan-defined neuromuscular stimulus. Otherwise allow a practical
  self-selected range and keep power/intensity purpose primary.

## Power Variability And Interval Architecture

Average power, normalized power, total load, and total time in a broad zone do
not fully describe the cycling stimulus.

- Inspect the distribution and sequence of work: power and duration of efforts,
  recovery power and duration, repetition count, accumulated work, coasting or
  zero-power time, and cadence/torque context.
- At the same average power, variable cycling can create a different
  physiological demand from constant cycling, particularly when fluctuations
  repeatedly cross an intensity-domain boundary. Do not classify a stochastic
  outdoor ride as equivalent to steady VT1 from average power or XSS alone.
- For an intended steady aerobic session, prefer execution and routes that
  limit unnecessary surges, repeated high-torque accelerations, and excessive
  coasting. Report unavoidable terrain or traffic variability instead of
  scoring it as poor pacing without context.
- For a planned stochastic, race-specific, over-under, micro-interval, or VO2Max
  session, preserve the intended variability. Do not smooth the prescription
  into an easier constant-power equivalent merely because average power matches.
- Judge an interval session from the complete work-recovery architecture and
  actual completed repetitions. High pulse, high source-reported VE, peak
  power, or total time alone does not establish the intended stimulus.
- Short and long intervals can produce different acute systemic and local
  responses. Do not claim that one format is universally superior. Let the
  active plan choose the role and structure, then calibrate it against this
  athlete's execution, physiological response, RPE, and subsequent recovery.
- When comparing sessions, match or disclose differences in interval structure,
  recovery intensity, total work, pacing mode, accumulated prior work, cadence,
  environment, and fueling before inferring adaptation.

## Thermal Strain

Treat thermal strain as a coupled athlete-environment response, not as ambient
temperature alone.

- Keep air temperature, relative humidity, dew point or vapor pressure,
  airflow, radiant exposure, skin temperature, core-temperature estimate,
  hydration, and thermal perception separate.
- Distinguish room or outdoor conditions from the microclimate at the body.
  Indoor fan placement can materially change strain without changing the room
  sensor.
- Interpret elevated HR/W, BR/W, VE/W, RPE, and altered SmO2 in thermal
  context. Do not label them loss of fitness or incomplete recovery when heat
  is a plausible contributor.
- Do not attribute drift causally to heat from temperature context alone.
  Require aligned thermal, physiological, temporal, and subjective evidence,
  and state confidence.
- Do not use one universal core-temperature value as both a performance rule
  and medical safety diagnosis. Preserve device type, measured versus
  estimated temperature, lag, placement, symptoms, and safety context.
- Separate heat acclimation from ordinary endurance prescription. Heat is an
  added stress: specify purpose, exposure dose, cooling or airflow choice,
  hydration plan, and stop conditions rather than treating reduced cooling as
  free aerobic stimulus.
- Compare heat response only across sufficiently matched workload, duration,
  cooling, hydration, clothing, and environment.

## Carbohydrate Availability And Fueling Context

Fueling is both an intervention and a confounder in cycling analysis.

- Protect quality and long-session execution with adequate carbohydrate unless
  low availability is an explicit, deliberate plan intervention.
- Do not prescribe low-carbohydrate availability merely because a session is
  easy, aerobic, fasted, early, or intended to improve fat oxidation.
- When late-session power, HR/W, BR/W, VE/W, RPE, SmO2, or durability differs,
  compare pre-ride intake, on-bike carbohydrate, elapsed time, fluid intake,
  heat, and likely glycogen context before attributing the change to fitness.
- Distinguish logged intake, confirmed consumed intake, and inferred
  carbohydrate availability. EatMyRide owns product and food-plan field
  semantics; this reference owns only the cross-source analysis context.
- Do not diagnose glycogen depletion from cardiac drift, respiratory behavior,
  SmO2, Garmin Stamina, or perceived fatigue alone.
- A deliberately low-carbohydrate session is not automatically a superior
  adaptation stimulus. Research does not establish a general performance
  advantage, while compromised high-intensity execution is a practical risk.
- Standardize fueling for durability comparisons where possible or label the
  comparison nutrition-confounded.

## Integrated Interpretation

For a long or composed cycling session:

1. Establish actual power structure, accumulated work, and timing.
2. Identify matched early and late work windows.
3. Verify sensor coverage and comparable setup.
4. Describe mechanical and physiological change separately.
5. Examine thermal and fueling context as potential contributors.
6. Assess whether a fresh intensity boundary may have shifted during the ride.
7. Separate observations, model outputs, and causal hypotheses.
8. Require repeated matched evidence before changing a progression anchor.

### Known Evidence Gaps

- No universal field metric or cutoff establishes durability across athletes,
  session types, environments, and fueling strategies.
- Threshold deterioration does not identify whether its primary contributor was
  central, muscular, thermal, nutritional, biomechanical, or motivational.
- Wearable BR, VE, SmO2, core-temperature estimates, and source-model outputs
  have device- and protocol-specific uncertainty. Correlated streams are not
  automatically independent confirmation.
- CP and W-prime recovery models do not perfectly predict intermittent exercise
  tolerance, especially without athlete-specific calibration.
- Cadence and torque effects depend on power, fatigue state, gradient, position,
  individual preference, and protocol. Evidence does not support one universal
  optimal cadence or a generic low-cadence training benefit.
- No single variability statistic captures interval architecture or proves the
  physiological cost of an outdoor ride. Inspect the underlying sequence.
- Ordinary activity streams cannot diagnose glycogen depletion or heat illness.
